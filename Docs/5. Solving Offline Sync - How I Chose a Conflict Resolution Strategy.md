# Solving Offline Sync: How I Chose a Conflict Resolution Strategy

**By Leo Bacevicius**

## Introduction

Here's something I hadn't thought much about before building an iOS app: what happens when the internet disappears?

As a designer, I've spent years crafting interfaces, obsessing over spacing, and debating button colors. Network connectivity? That was always someone else's problem. The app sends data to the server, the server responds, the UI updates. Simple.

Then I started building Plank Challenge—a fitness app where users track daily planks—and reality hit me. People plank in their basements. In airplane mode. At gyms with terrible WiFi. On morning commutes through tunnels.

If my app required constant connectivity, it would fail precisely when people needed it most: during their workout.

This is the story of how I went from "I'll figure out sync later" to understanding one of mobile development's most nuanced problems—and how a competitive analysis helped me find the right solution.

## The Moment It Clicked

I was testing the app on my commute, planking in my living room (as one does), when I noticed something. My phone had lost signal. The plank completed, the celebration animation played, but... did it save?

I stared at the screen, suddenly aware of a gap in my thinking. What happens to this plank session? Where does it go? What if I do another plank before getting signal back? What if I open the app on my iPad while my phone is still offline?

These questions cascaded into more questions:

- What if two devices create planks for the same day?
- What if I delete a plank on one device while offline?
- What if the server has data my phone doesn't know about?
- What if my phone has data the server has never seen?

I realized I had been thinking about my app as a single-device experience. But modern users don't live on one device. They have phones, tablets, sometimes multiple phones. They expect their data to follow them everywhere, seamlessly, invisibly.

This is the sync problem. And it's harder than it looks.

## Understanding the Problem Space

Before diving into solutions, I needed to understand what "sync" actually means. After some research, I identified the core challenges:

### The Fundamental Tension

There's an inherent conflict between two desirable properties:

1. **Availability**: The app should always work, even offline
2. **Consistency**: All devices should see the same data

You can't have perfect versions of both. If the app works offline, it can create data that other devices don't know about. If you require online connectivity for consistency, availability suffers.

This is actually a well-known principle in distributed systems called the CAP theorem. I didn't know that term when I started—I just knew something felt impossible.

### Types of Conflicts

I mapped out the scenarios that could cause problems:

| Scenario | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Create-Create** | Same item created on two devices |
| **Update-Update** | Same item modified on two devices |
| **Update-Delete** | One device updates, another deletes |
| **Delete-Delete** | Both devices delete (usually harmless) |

For a plank tracking app, the most common scenario would be Create-Create: someone does a plank on their phone while offline, then does another on their iPad. Both devices have new data the other doesn't know about.

### The Stakes

Getting this wrong has real consequences:

- **Data loss**: User's workout history disappears
- **Duplicate data**: Same plank appears twice
- **Corrupted state**: Streak calculations become wrong
- **User confusion**: "Why doesn't my iPad show my morning plank?"
- **Trust erosion**: Users stop relying on the app

I couldn't afford to get this wrong. People's streaks—their hard-won consistency—depended on reliable sync.

## Competitive Analysis: How Others Solve This

Rather than inventing something new, I decided to study how established apps handle offline sync. I looked at fitness apps, productivity tools, and note-taking apps—any category where offline support matters.

### Apple Health

**Approach**: Last Write Wins + Merging

Apple Health accepts data from multiple sources (Apple Watch, iPhone, third-party apps) and merges it together. For metrics like steps, it intelligently deduplicates. For discrete events like workouts, each entry is treated as unique.

**Key insight**: Workouts are discrete events with unique identifiers. Two workouts are never "the same workout edited differently"—they're just two workouts.

### Strava

**Approach**: Immutable Activities

Strava treats each activity (run, ride, etc.) as an immutable event. Once recorded, activities can be deleted but not fundamentally edited (you can change the title, but not the GPS data). This sidesteps most conflicts—you can't have an update-update conflict on something that can't be updated.

**Key insight**: If data is append-only, conflicts become much simpler. You're only ever adding, never modifying the same thing.

### Notion / Google Docs

**Approach**: Operational Transformation / CRDTs

Collaborative document editors use sophisticated algorithms to merge concurrent edits. If two people type in the same paragraph simultaneously, the system merges both contributions intelligently.

**Key insight**: This is massive overkill for a fitness app. I'm not building collaborative editing—I'm tracking individual workout events.

### Todoist

**Approach**: Last Write Wins with Timestamps

Todoist uses timestamps to determine which change "wins" when conflicts occur. The most recent change overwrites earlier ones. Simple, predictable, occasionally loses data.

**Key insight**: Last Write Wins is simple but can cause data loss if not careful. The user who saved last wins, even if their version is worse.

### Day One (Journal App)

**Approach**: Create-Only with UUIDs

Day One generates a unique ID for each journal entry on the device. Entries sync by ID—if the server hasn't seen that ID, it's a new entry. Entries can be edited, with last-write-wins for edits, but creation never conflicts.

**Key insight**: If every item has a globally unique ID generated at creation time, you can never have create-create conflicts. Both "new" items are genuinely new and distinct.

### Things 3

**Approach**: Custom Sync Engine

Things built their own sync system called "Things Cloud" that handles offline gracefully. Tasks created offline get UUIDs immediately. When back online, they sync without conflicts because each task is unique.

**Key insight**: The task management domain is similar to workout tracking—discrete items created by one user, synced across their devices.

## Patterns Emerging

After studying these apps, I noticed a pattern. The apps with the smoothest sync experiences shared certain characteristics:

### 1. Immutable or Append-Only Data

The easiest data to sync is data that never changes. If you only ever add new items (never edit existing ones), you eliminate an entire class of conflicts.

### 2. Client-Generated UUIDs

Rather than waiting for the server to assign IDs, successful apps generate UUIDs on the device immediately. This means:
- Items have identity even before syncing
- The same item synced twice is recognized as the same item (idempotency)
- No waiting for network to "create" something

### 3. Discrete Events vs. Shared Documents

Fitness tracking, task management, and journaling all deal with discrete events owned by a single user. This is fundamentally different from collaborative documents where multiple people edit the same thing.

### 4. Clear Ownership

Each item belongs to one user. There's no shared state between users that could conflict. My planks are mine; your planks are yours.

## Applying This to Plank Challenge

With these patterns in mind, I analyzed my specific domain.

### What is a Plank Session?

A plank session is:
- A **discrete event** (not a document that evolves)
- **Created once** (you did a plank at a specific time for a specific duration)
- **Owned by one user** (no collaboration)
- **Rarely edited** (maybe you'd correct a typo in notes, but the core data is fixed)
- **Occasionally deleted** (mistakes happen)

This profile matches Apple Health and Strava more than Notion or Google Docs.

### My Data Model

Each plank session has:
- `clientId`: UUID generated on the device at creation time
- `serverId`: UUID assigned by server after sync (for server-side references)
- `duration`: How long the plank lasted
- `plankType`: Elbow, straight arm, or parallettes
- `performedAt`: When the plank happened
- `inputMethod`: Timer or manual entry

The critical field is `clientId`. This is generated the moment the user completes a plank, before any network activity. It's the item's true identity.

### Why Conflicts Don't Really Exist

Here's the key realization: **in my domain, there are no conflicts**.

Think about it:
- If I plank on my iPhone at 8am and on my iPad at 8pm, those are two different planks. Both are valid. Both should sync.
- If I plank on my iPhone and my iPad at the exact same time... well, I'm a talented planker, and both are still valid.
- I can't "edit the same plank differently on two devices" because plank data is essentially immutable after creation.

The only real conflict scenario is deletion:
- What if I delete a plank on device A while offline, but device B doesn't know yet?

For this, the solution is simple: the deletion syncs when device A comes online, and device B pulls the deletion on its next sync. The plank is gone from both.

### The Event-Sourcing Approach

Instead of thinking about "syncing state," I reframed it as "syncing events."

Each plank is an event that happened. The server collects all events from all devices. The current state (streak count, total planks, etc.) is computed from the event history.

```
Device A creates plank (clientId: abc-123) → Event
Device B creates plank (clientId: xyz-789) → Event
Both sync → Server has both events
Server computes: streak = 1, totalPlanks = 2
```

If the same `clientId` arrives twice (network retry, bug, whatever), the server simply ignores the duplicate. This is idempotency—the same operation can be performed multiple times without changing the result.

### Handling Deletion

I made a product decision that simplified things further: **users can only delete planks from the current day**.

Why? A few reasons:
- Prevents gaming the streak system (delete "bad" days retroactively)
- Reduces complexity (deletion history doesn't need to span weeks)
- Matches user intent (deletions are usually "oops, I didn't mean to log that")

For today's planks, deletion is simple:
- Mark the plank as deleted locally
- Sync the deletion to the server
- Other devices pull the deletion and remove their local copy

No conflicts because:
- Only the owner can delete their planks
- Deletions don't conflict with each other (deleting twice = still deleted)
- Deletions don't conflict with edits (we don't really edit planks)

## The Solution: Last Write Wins (But With a Twist)

After all this analysis, I landed on a strategy I'd call "Last Write Wins with Event Sourcing."

But here's the twist: **there's nothing to "win."**

Traditional Last Write Wins implies a conflict where the latest change overwrites earlier ones. In my system, each plank is a unique event. There's no competition between writes because they're not writing to the same thing.

The "last write wins" part only applies to the rare edge cases:
- User profile updates (last profile save wins)
- Plank deletion (last deletion state wins, but deletion is final anyway)

For the core use case—logging planks—every write wins because every write is unique.

### Implementation Summary

Here's how it works in practice:

**On plank completion:**
1. Generate `clientId` (UUID) immediately
2. Save to local database with `syncStatus: pending`
3. If online, attempt sync immediately
4. If offline, queue for later

**On sync:**
1. POST plank to server with `clientId`
2. Server checks if `clientId` exists
3. If new: insert, return `serverId`
4. If duplicate: return existing record (idempotent)
5. Mark local plank as `synced`

**On pull (from server):**
1. GET all planks modified since last sync
2. For each remote plank:
   - If `clientId` exists locally: update `serverId`, mark synced
   - If new: create local copy (came from another device)
3. Update last sync timestamp

**On deletion:**
1. Check plank is from today (reject if older)
2. Mark local plank as `deleted`
3. Sync deletion to server
4. Server soft-deletes the record
5. Other devices pull the deletion

## What I Learned

This journey taught me several things:

### 1. Domain Matters

The right sync strategy depends entirely on what you're syncing. Collaborative documents need sophisticated merge algorithms. Fitness events need unique identifiers and append-only thinking. Don't copy Notion's approach for a workout app.

### 2. Simplicity Wins

The most elegant solutions often come from reframing the problem. Instead of "how do I merge conflicting plank edits," I asked "why would plank edits ever conflict?" The answer was: they don't, if you model them correctly.

### 3. UUIDs Are Magic

Client-generated UUIDs solve so many problems. Items have identity before they exist on the server. Retries are automatically idempotent. Offline-created items don't need special handling.

### 4. Competitive Analysis Works

I didn't invent anything new. I studied how proven apps handle this problem, extracted patterns, and applied them to my domain. Standing on the shoulders of giants, as they say.

### 5. Product Decisions Enable Technical Solutions

The "delete only today's planks" rule wasn't a technical limitation—it was a product choice that made the technical implementation simpler. Good product thinking and good engineering often reinforce each other.

## Looking Forward

With sync strategy decided, I can now build confidently. The backend will use Cloudflare Workers with D1 (SQLite) for storage. The iOS app will use SwiftData for local persistence with a custom sync engine.

Users will be able to:
- Plank anywhere, anytime, regardless of connectivity
- See their data instantly on all their devices
- Trust that nothing is lost

And I'll be able to sleep at night knowing that someone planking in an underground bunker will eventually have their effort recognized when they resurface.

That's the goal, anyway. Implementation is next.
