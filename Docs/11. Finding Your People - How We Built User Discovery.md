# Finding Your People: How We Built User Discovery

**By Leo Bacevicius**

## Introduction

Fitness is personal, but it doesn't have to be lonely.

One of the most powerful motivators in any habit-building app isn't badges or streaks—it's other people. Seeing that your friend just completed their morning plank. Getting a nudge of friendly competition when someone passes your streak. Knowing that you're not the only one trying to build this habit.

But here's the challenge: how do you help users find the right people to connect with?

Phase 5 of Plank Challenge tackled this question. We already had the basics—following other users, seeing their profiles, searching by name. What we didn't have was that moment when the app says "Hey, you might want to follow this person." That intelligent suggestion that makes a social network feel alive instead of empty.

## The Cold Start Problem

Every social app faces the same chicken-and-egg challenge. Users won't engage if there's no one to connect with. But there's no one to connect with until users engage.

Instagram solves this by importing your phone contacts. Twitter suggests celebrities and news outlets. LinkedIn mines your email history. These approaches work, but they feel invasive—and for a focused fitness app, they're overkill.

I wanted something simpler. Something that didn't require access to contacts or email. Something that could make smart suggestions based purely on what happens inside the app.

## What Makes a Good Suggestion?

Before building anything, I spent time thinking about what would make me actually tap "Follow" on a suggested user. Three scenarios came to mind:

**The Friend-of-Friend**
"Sarah follows this person, and I trust Sarah's taste." This is the social proof that makes recommendations feel credible. If people I already follow are following someone, there's probably a reason.

**The Kindred Spirit**
"This person has a 14-day streak, and so do I." There's something encouraging about finding others at a similar point in their journey. Not the influencer with a 500-day streak who makes you feel inadequate, but someone fighting the same battles you are.

**The Active Community Member**
"This person planked yesterday." A ghost town is depressing. Suggestions should prioritize people who are actually using the app, not accounts that signed up six months ago and never came back.

## The Algorithm Behind the Magic

With those principles in mind, I designed a scoring system. Each potential suggestion gets points based on multiple factors:

| What We Check | Why It Matters |
|---------------|----------------|
| **Followed by your friends** | Social proof is powerful. If multiple people you trust follow someone, they're probably worth following. |
| **Similar streak level** | Connecting with peers at your level is more motivating than comparing yourself to legends. |
| **Recently active** | No one wants to follow an abandoned account. Active users make the community feel alive. |
| **Generally popular** | As a fallback, popularity is a signal of quality. High follower counts mean others found value there. |

The system weighs these factors, with friend-of-friend connections counting the most. Someone followed by three of your friends will rank higher than someone who's just generically popular.

## The Suggestion Reasons

One detail I'm particularly proud of: every suggestion comes with an explanation.

Instead of just showing a list of faces, the app tells you *why* each person is suggested:

- "Followed by someone you follow"
- "Followed by 3 people you follow"
- "Similar streak level"
- "Recently active"
- "Popular in the community"

This transparency does two things. First, it makes the suggestions feel less creepy—you understand how the app arrived at this recommendation. Second, it helps you make better decisions. "Followed by 3 people you follow" is a stronger signal than "Popular in the community," and now you can see that.

## What We Explicitly Don't Do

Sometimes the most important design decisions are about what you choose *not* to build.

**We don't suggest yourself.** Sounds obvious, but it's an easy bug to introduce. The algorithm explicitly filters you out.

**We don't suggest people you already follow.** Again obvious, but critical. Nothing breaks trust faster than suggesting someone who's already in your following list.

**We don't use external data.** No contacts, no email parsing, no social media cross-referencing. Your suggestions come purely from what you do inside Plank Challenge. This is both a privacy feature and a simplicity choice.

**We don't show deleted accounts.** If someone leaves the platform, they disappear from suggestions immediately. No zombie accounts cluttering the results.

## The Power of "Similar Streak Level"

I want to highlight this factor specifically, because it's where fitness apps often get it wrong.

Imagine you're on day 5 of building a plank habit. You open the app and see suggested users with 200-day streaks, thousands of planks, holding positions for ten minutes at a time. How does that make you feel?

For most people: inadequate. Maybe even discouraged. "I'll never be like that."

Now imagine you see someone on day 7. Someone who, like you, is just starting out. Someone whose progress you could realistically match or even surpass. That's motivating. That's a peer, not a celebrity.

The algorithm knows your current streak and looks for users within a reasonable range—roughly 50% higher or lower, with a minimum range so brand-new users still get suggestions. A user on day 10 sees suggestions in the 5-15 day range. A user on day 100 sees suggestions in the 50-150 range.

The goal isn't to show you the "best" users. It's to show you *your* people.

## The Edge Cases

Building this feature surfaced some interesting scenarios:

**Brand new user, follows nobody**
The friend-of-friend logic can't help here—there are no friends yet. So we fall back to recently active users and popular accounts. It's a reasonable starting point until they build their own network.

**Power user who follows everyone**
If you already follow most of the active users, suggestions become harder. The algorithm gracefully degrades to showing whoever remains, prioritized by activity and popularity.

**User with a 0-day streak**
Everyone starts somewhere. We still suggest users with low streaks (0-3 days), so new users don't feel excluded from the similarity matching.

## What Comes Next

User discovery is just one piece of the social puzzle. Having suggestions is great, but they need a place to live in the UI. The iOS app will need:

- A "Discover" or "Suggestions" section, probably on the social tab
- The ability to quickly follow from the suggestion list
- Pull-to-refresh to get new suggestions
- Perhaps a "Not interested" option to refine recommendations over time

That last point—learning from dismissals—isn't built yet. It's a future enhancement. For now, the suggestions are purely additive, using positive signals rather than learning from rejections.

## Reflections: Social Features Are Hard

I've worked on many technical features in this project, but social features require a different kind of thinking. The technical implementation is straightforward. The *design* of what to show, when, and why—that's where the real work happens.

Every social app is making implicit statements about human connection. "These people are worth your attention." "These relationships matter." Getting that wrong doesn't just create a bad user experience; it can make people feel excluded or inadequate.

I think about this a lot with Plank Challenge. This isn't Instagram where follower counts are social currency. It's a fitness app where the goal is to help people build a habit. The social features should support that goal—encouraging without pressuring, connecting without comparing.

User discovery is one small piece of that vision. Show people others who are like them. Let them find encouragement in shared struggle. Make the journey feel less solitary.

Because ultimately, the best motivation isn't a badge or a streak counter. It's knowing that someone else is doing this too.
