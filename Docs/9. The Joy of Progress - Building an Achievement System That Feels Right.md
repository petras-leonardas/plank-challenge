# The Joy of Progress: Building an Achievement System That Feels Right

**By Leo Bacevicius**

## Introduction

There's a moment in every fitness app when you realize you've been at it for a while. You check your history, see thirty planks logged over the past month, and think: "Huh. That's... something."

But "something" isn't satisfying. It doesn't make you want to come back tomorrow. It doesn't give you a reason to tell your friend about the app. It's just data sitting there, waiting to mean something.

Phase 3 of Plank Challenge was about giving that data meaning. I built an achievement system—badges, milestones, notifications, progress tracking—that transforms raw plank counts into moments of recognition. And along the way, I learned why the details of how you celebrate matter almost as much as what you're celebrating.

## Why Achievements Are a Design Problem, Not Just a Coding Problem

Before writing any backend logic, I needed to answer a design question: what makes an achievement feel earned?

I've used apps where achievements feel hollow. "You logged in 7 days in a row!" Thanks, but I didn't really *do* anything. I just opened an app. Other apps get it right—unlocking a badge in Duolingo or completing a ring in Apple Fitness feels like a genuine accomplishment.

The difference comes down to three things:

| Bad Achievement | Good Achievement |
|-----------------|------------------|
| Rewards passive behavior | Rewards genuine effort |
| Arbitrary thresholds (why 7? why not 8?) | Meaningful milestones tied to real progress |
| Announced once, then forgotten | Part of a visible progression system |
| Same reward for everyone | Reflects your personal journey |

For Plank Challenge, I wanted achievements that respected the user's effort. Holding a plank for three minutes is genuinely hard. Maintaining a 30-day streak requires real commitment. These deserve recognition.

## The Badge Taxonomy: Four Types of Achievement

After sketching dozens of potential badges, I organized them into four categories:

### Streak Badges
These celebrate consistency. The hardest part of fitness isn't the exercise—it's showing up day after day.

| Badge | Milestone |
|-------|-----------|
| Week Warrior | 7-day streak |
| Fortnight Fighter | 14-day streak |
| Monthly Master | 30-day streak |
| Quarter Champion | 90-day streak |
| Year-Long Legend | 365-day streak |

### Count Badges
These celebrate volume. Some people plank once a day; others do multiple sessions. Both approaches deserve recognition.

| Badge | Milestone |
|-------|-----------|
| First Plank | 1 plank completed |
| Century Club | 100 planks completed |
| Plank Master | 500 planks completed |
| Plank Legend | 1,000 planks completed |

### Duration Badges
These celebrate intensity. Holding a plank for one minute is a starting point. Holding it for ten minutes is an athletic achievement.

| Badge | Milestone |
|-------|-----------|
| One Minute Wonder | 60-second plank |
| Three Minute Champion | 180-second plank |
| Iron Core | 600-second (10-minute) plank |

### Special Badges
These celebrate the unexpected—the person who planks at 5am before the sun rises, or the one who tries every plank variation in the app.

| Badge | Milestone |
|-------|-----------|
| Early Bird | Plank before 6 AM |
| Night Owl | Plank after 11 PM |
| Perfect Week | 7 consecutive days |
| Variety Pack | Try all five plank types |

Twenty-five badges in total. Enough to create a long-term progression, but not so many that they become meaningless.

## The Invisible Problem: Awarding Badges at the Right Moment

Here's something I didn't anticipate: badges need to be awarded at exactly the right moment, and that moment is harder to identify than you'd think.

Consider the "Three Minute Champion" badge. You'd think the logic is simple: "If plank duration >= 180 seconds, award badge."

But when do you check? After every plank is created? What if the user earned the badge months ago but the system wasn't checking back then? Do they just... never get it?

This led me to an important architectural decision: badge checking should be *retroactive*. When we check for new badges, we look at the user's entire history—not just their latest action.

For most badges, this means checking aggregate statistics:
- Longest streak ever achieved
- Total planks completed
- Longest single plank duration

For special badges like "Early Bird," it meant querying the database for any plank performed in the early morning hours, regardless of when it happened.

This approach ensures that if someone was planking for months before the badge system existed, they'd receive all the badges they'd already earned the moment they trigger a new badge check.

## The Notification Decision: Celebrate Without Annoying

Badges are meaningless if users don't know they earned them. But notifications are a minefield. Nothing kills an app's reputation faster than becoming "that app that won't stop notifying me."

I landed on these principles:

### In-App Notifications First
Every badge earned creates an in-app notification. These appear in a notification center within the app, not as push notifications that interrupt your day.

### Push Notifications Are Earned
Push notifications are reserved for high-signal events:
- Major streak milestones (30 days, 90 days, 365 days)
- Streak at risk (you haven't planked today)
- Achievements that take real time to earn

I haven't implemented push notifications yet—that's Phase 7—but the notification infrastructure is designed for this distinction. Each notification has a type, and the type determines how aggressively it should be surfaced.

### Notifications Have a Shelf Life
Here's something I almost got wrong: notifications accumulate forever unless you clean them up. A user who's been active for a year could have thousands of notifications, making the feature useless.

I added an automatic cleanup policy: read notifications older than 90 days get deleted. The system also caps notifications at 500 per user. Old noise disappears; recent achievements stay visible.

## The Progress Dashboard: Making Data Meaningful

Raw statistics are boring. "Total plank time: 7,430 seconds" doesn't feel like an achievement. It feels like a math problem.

The progress system transforms statistics into stories:

### Calendar View (Last 90 Days)
Every day you planked shows up as an active cell. You can see your consistency visually—the gaps where life got in the way, the stretches where you were crushing it.

### Weekly Trends (Last 12 Weeks)
How many planks did you do each week? How many minutes? Are you trending up or down? This view shows patterns that daily data obscures.

### Milestone Progress
Instead of showing raw numbers, the progress dashboard shows how close you are to the next meaningful milestone:

| Category | Next Goal | Progress |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| Streak | Monthly Master (30 days) | 47% |
| Total Planks | Century Club (100 planks) | 78% |
| Duration | Five Minute Fighter (300s) | 65% |

This reframes the statistics as a journey with clear destinations.

## A Lesson in Single Source of Truth

While building the progress dashboard, I made a mistake that taught me an important architectural lesson.

I defined milestone thresholds in two places: once in the badge definitions (where "Monthly Master" is defined as 30 days), and again in the progress dashboard (where I hardcoded the milestones for display).

This worked until I decided to add a new streak badge. I added it to the badge definitions... and forgot to add it to the progress dashboard. The badge existed, but the progress system didn't know about it.

The fix was to make the badge definitions the single source of truth. The progress dashboard now derives its milestones directly from the badge system. Add a new badge? It automatically appears in progress tracking. Change a threshold? It updates everywhere.

This principle—define data once, derive everything else—sounds obvious in retrospect. But it's easy to violate when you're moving fast.

## The Optimization That Mattered

After building the badge and progress systems, I ran into a performance problem.

Every time a user completed a plank, the system was making several sequential database queries: fetch user stats, fetch existing badges, check for special badge eligibility, fetch plank types for the variety badge. Each query was fast, but they added up.

The fix was to batch these queries. Instead of making them one after another, the system now sends all queries in a single batch, and the database returns all results together. The time dropped significantly—what used to take multiple round trips to the database now takes one.

For users creating planks, this means the app feels snappier. The badge check happens in the background, but a faster background still means a more responsive experience.

## What I Learned About Motivation

Building the achievement system taught me something about why people stick with habit apps.

The obvious answer is: people like achievements. Badges are fun. Progress bars are satisfying. But I think it goes deeper than that.

**Achievements create memory anchors.** When you earn a badge, you remember the moment. "I was in that hotel room in Chicago when I hit my 30-day streak." The badge transforms a forgettable Tuesday plank into a memorable milestone.

**Progress tracking validates effort.** When you see a calendar with 45 active days over the past 90, you have proof that you've been doing something meaningful. The data says: "You showed up. Consistently. That matters."

**Milestones create micro-goals.** Instead of the vague goal of "get fitter," you have concrete next steps: reach 100 planks, hit a 2-minute plank, complete a perfect week. These smaller goals are achievable, and achieving them builds momentum.

## The Designer's Reflection

Phase 3 was satisfying in a way that previous phases weren't. Authentication is invisible when it works—users don't celebrate a successful login. The sync engine is similarly invisible—you only notice it when it breaks.

But achievements are visible. They're the parts of the app users show their friends. "Look, I hit a 30-day streak!" That visibility makes the design work tangible.

At the same time, I'm aware of the responsibility that comes with gamification. Achievement systems can become manipulative—designed to create compulsion rather than genuine satisfaction. Every design decision I made, I asked myself: does this celebrate the user's real accomplishment, or does it manufacture artificial urgency?

I think I landed in the right place. The badges are hard to earn. The milestones are meaningful. The system celebrates what the user actually did, not what I want them to do tomorrow.

## What's Next

Phase 3 is complete. Users can earn badges, track progress, and receive notifications when they hit milestones. The infrastructure is optimized, the code is clean, and everything is deployed.

Phase 4 will add profile images and media—letting users personalize their accounts with avatars. It's a different kind of feature, less about gamification and more about identity.

But the achievement system will keep evolving. I already have ideas for future badges: consistency badges for planking at the same time every day, improvement badges for beating your personal best, social badges for group challenges. The foundation is built; the possibilities are wide open.

For now, though, I'm going to plank. I'm three days away from my Week Warrior badge, and I've got a streak to protect.
